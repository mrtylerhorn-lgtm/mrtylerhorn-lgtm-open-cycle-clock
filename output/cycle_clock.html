Where is the US economy in its cycle, and what has that meant for stocks? This is an open-source rebuild of the Market Cycle Clock that the research firm RenMac publishes each month. It places every month since on a grid using two "hands": how fast the economy is growing, and whether inflation is speeding up or slowing down.

    How it works

    The idea. Stock prices react less to whether the economy is good or bad than to whether things are getting better or worse, and to how the Federal Reserve is likely to respond. When inflation is cooling, the Fed has room to cut rates. When it is heating up, the Fed leans against it. The clock puts growth and inflation on one picture so you can see which combination you are in and what has followed it before.

    What RenMac has said about its version. Jeff deGraaf's clock puts inflation on the vertical axis and growth on the horizontal, divides each into thirds, and scores the nine cells by what stocks, sectors and bond yields did next, using data back to 1964. He has said the inputs do not lean on CPI, that energy prices drive much of the inflation signal, that the direction of inflation matters more than its level, and that inputs are chosen for their effect on the stock market rather than on the economy. In a Mauldin Economics interview (December 2025) he argued that inflation matters more to stock returns than growth does, and that weak growth often comes before better returns because it invites policy support.

    The two hands, in detail
    • Growth hand. The average of nine inputs, each compared with its own history: industrial production, payroll jobs, real household income excluding government payments, real business sales and housing starts (each versus a year ago); the 12-month change in the unemployment rate and in jobless claims (counted as strength when they fall); and the Philadelphia Fed's survey of manufacturers' activity and new orders, the longest free stand-in for the ISM survey.
    • Inflation hand. The average of five inputs that measure whether inflation is speeding up: oil's price change over a year, and the six-month change in the yearly inflation rate for producer commodity prices, consumer prices, core consumer prices and hourly wages. Inflation can be high and still score as "cooling" if it is falling.
    • Each hand is smoothed with a three-month average and rescaled so that 1 is a typical swing. A cell border sits wherever a third of all months fall below it.
    • Scoring starts in , when every input exists. RenMac's history starts in 1964.
    How the results are measured
    • Stocks are the whole US market including dividends (CRSP value-weighted index, from the Ken French Data Library), not just the S&P 500. Cash is the one-month Treasury bill.
    • Data for a month usually arrive during the next month, so a reading for month t is treated as known at the end of month t+1. Real business sales arrive about a month later than that, so they enter the clock a month late. "Next 12 months" runs from month t+2 to t+13. "Next month" is the return in month t+2, times 12 to show it at a yearly pace.
    • The "How sure" range is a 90% confidence range for the long-run average, not for any single year. Because 12-month windows overlap, a cell's months come from far fewer independent years than months; the ranges use Newey-West standard errors to allow for that.
    • Yields and the Fed funds rate are monthly averages, compared from the month the data come out to six months later.
    • Industries are Ken French's 12 industry groups ("Technology" is his business-equipment group). Cyclicals are cars and durables, manufacturing and technology; defensives are staples, utilities and health care. These groups were chosen by how each industry's relative return tracks real GDP growth (see the report card). Retail was dropped from the cyclicals because it behaves defensively by that measure.
    Limits you should know about
    • The data are today's revised figures, not what was published at the time. Payrolls and income in particular get revised, so past readings look cleaner than they would have in real time. A true test would use the Fed's ALFRED archive of first-published data.
    • The main view sets the thirds using the full history, which uses hindsight. The real-time column and the trading test remove that.
    • Nine cells, three horizons and two hands make dozens of comparisons. A few will look strong by chance, so trust patterns that show up in both halves of the record and in the real-time version, not single striking cells.
    • The Philadelphia Fed survey was added in an earlier revision partly because it brought this clock closer to RenMac's public readings. That is a mild form of fitting to a handful of data points, and today's cell depends on it (see "How firm is today's reading?").
    • This is history, not a forecast, and not investment advice.
    Glossary
    Cyclicals
    Industries whose profits swing with the economy, such as carmakers, manufacturers, tech and retailers.
    Defensives
    Industries people keep paying for in bad times: groceries and household goods, utilities, health care.
    Beat cash by
    Stocks' return minus what a Treasury bill paid over the same time. It shows the reward for taking stock-market risk.
    10-year yield
    The interest rate on 10-year US government bonds. "bp" means basis points; 100 bp is one percentage point.
    Fed funds rate
    The overnight interest rate the Federal Reserve targets. "pt" means percentage points.
    Percentile
    Where a reading ranks among all past months. 76th percentile means at or above about 76% of them.
    Standard error
    How much an average would wobble from luck alone. A gap of about 2 standard errors or more is the usual bar for "probably not luck".
    Z-score
    How far a number is from its long-run average, measured in typical swings. +2 is unusual; +3 is rare.
